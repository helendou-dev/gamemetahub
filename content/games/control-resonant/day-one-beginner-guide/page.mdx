Dylan Faden is not his sister. Where Jesse Faden walked into the Oldest House with a transforming Service Weapon and a bureau director's badge, Dylan wakes from a years-long coma with a shapeshifting melee weapon bonded to his chest and a warped Manhattan collapsing around him. CONTROL Resonant is Remedy's most aggressive game yet: no block, no cover, no traditional parry, and a combat loop that only rewards players who stay on the offensive.

My first two hours were a mess. I tried to play it like the original Control, floating at mid-range and picking enemies off. I died. I tried to tank hits with a heavy hammer form. I died faster. It was only when I stopped running and started chaining the core loop — Attack, Power, Ability, Falter, Execute, Dominance — that the game clicked. Below is everything I wish I had known before starting, organized so you can read it in order or jump to the system that is confusing you right now.

## Do You Need to Play the Original Control?

No. Resonant is designed as both a sequel and an entry point. The relationship between Dylan and Jesse is explained inside the story, and the game reintroduces the Federal Bureau of Control and the concept of Objects of Power in the opening prologue. Playing the 2019 Control enriches the lore callbacks, but it is not required.

That said, the tone is different. The first game was a gun-heavy supernatural shooter with Metroidvania structure. Resonant is a melee action RPG with open districts, buildcraft, and optional boss hunting. If you are expecting Service Weapon forms at range, reset your expectations in the first ten minutes.

## The Core Combat Loop: Attack → Power → Ability → Falter → Execute → Dominance

Resonant's combat has one rule above all others: aggression generates resources, and resources generate damage. There is no block button. The only defensive tools are dashes, air dodges, and the Perfect Dodge, which slows time and sets up a heavy counter. Everything else is about pressing forward.

The loop works like this. You attack with the Aberrant to build Power. You spend Power on paranatural Combat Abilities. Those abilities build Falter, which is the stagger meter on enemies. When Falter fills, an execution mark appears. Executing a Faltered enemy grants Dominance, a temporary damage buff that lets you punish everything else in the room. Then you repeat the cycle.

In my experience, the most common beginner mistake is to treat abilities as an emergency button. They are not. Abilities are the Falter engine, and Falter is the gateway to Execute and Dominance. If you are sitting on a full Power bar because you are waiting for the perfect moment, you are playing too conservatively. Spend it. Force the stagger. The execution will refund your momentum.

Healing reinforces this philosophy. Most of your health recovery comes from blue orbs dropped by defeated enemies. The game wants you in the thick of the fight, because that is where the health drops. Passive or defensive play is punished twice: you generate Power slower, and you see fewer healing drops.

## The Aberrant Weapon System: Forms, Slots, and Builds

The Aberrant is the center of the entire game. It replaces Jesse's Service Weapon with a melee weapon that can shift between forms, each with its own moveset, speed, range, and talent tree. You equip three components at once: a Primary Form, a Secondary Form, and a Combo Ender. Mid-combo, the weapon can seamlessly transform between these slots, letting you open with fast strikes and finish with a heavy blow.

The forms seen in previews and early coverage include dual blades for rapid crit chains, a scythe for wide crowd control, an axe or hammer for single-target burst, a whip for range and pull, gauntlets for close-range brawling, and a drill-like form for sustained pressure. Each form has its own upgrade tree with improved power, stagger damage, status effects, and combo extensions.

My personal early setup was dual blades primary for Power generation, scythe secondary for crowd control, and hammer as the Combo Ender for executions. It is not the only viable combination, but it taught me the rhythm of the game without locking me into a single range or speed.

## Movement, Evasion, and Why Perfect Dodge Is Your Main Damage Source

Dylan's mobility is the other half of combat. The kit includes a dash, a double dash, a double jump, a short hover or levitate, an air dodge, and Shift, which reorients gravity so Dylan can walk on walls and ceilings. In open districts and boss arenas alike, standing still is the fastest way to lose health.

The most important tool is the Perfect Dodge. Dodge just before an attack connects and time slows, leaving the enemy vulnerable to a heavy counter. Multiple guides, including one from GuruGamer, describe this as your main damage source early on, and I agree. Large enemies telegraph their wind-ups clearly; once you learn the timing, you can turn every attack into an opening.

Gravity Shift is not just a traversal gimmick. In enclosed arenas like the Sinkhole mission, walking on walls and ceilings is required to reach weak points and escape area attacks. In combat, it also lets you reposition above groups or flank enemies that track horizontally. The first time I used it to reach a tentacle clinging to a ceiling, I understood why Remedy built the entire movement kit around verticality.

## The Gap: Your Build Hub and Why You Should Visit It Often

The Gap is a mental space that functions as your central hub. You can enter it freely outside of combat to respec abilities, swap Aberrant forms, craft and equip Artifacts, and test combos on a training arena. It is the closest thing Resonant has to a pause menu for buildcraft.

Artifacts provide passive bonuses and can be changed at any time. This matters because different zones and bosses favor different damage types and defenses. If a Resonant is throwing out heat-based projectiles, swapping in resistance Artifacts before the fight is often the difference between a close call and a clean kill. I made the mistake of ignoring Artifacts for the first three hours and then watched my damage spike once I started matching them to each encounter.

The training arena is also valuable. Some Combo Enders have longer wind-ups than they look, and some ability combinations lock you in place. Testing a new build on a dummy before walking into a boss room saves time and frustration.

## Zone XP, Resonant Surge, and the Open-World Structure

Resonant is split into large open districts. Instead of Dylan leveling in the traditional RPG sense, zones themselves gain XP from the activities you complete inside them. As a zone levels up, it unlocks improvements: better health drops, revealed loot locations, challenge cabinets, and talent points for Dylan.

This design means side content is not filler. It is the main source of your early power. Skipping zone activities leaves you under-leveled for the main story and missing quality-of-life upgrades that make combat smoother.

After you defeat a Resonant, the zone enters a Resonant Surge. Enemies become stronger and new threats appear, but side content refreshes. Most Resonants are optional after the first; you can choose the order in which to tackle them, though the first and last are fixed by the story. Each defeated Resonant unlocks a new paranatural ability chosen inside The Gap, often with branching options like a heat wave or an empowered heat strike.

## First Major Boss: How to Beat The Collective

The Collective is the mold-themed Resonant most commonly shown in early previews and coverage. It is a multi-phase fight that teaches you to use almost every tool in the opening kit: Gravity Shift, target prioritization, Falter execution, and crowd control.

You cannot damage the main body at the start. Focus on the tentacles attached to the walls and arena. Use Gravity Shift to walk on vertical surfaces and reach them. Build Falter on each tentacle, execute it for Dominance, and use the damage buff to pressure the next target. The main body will expose weak points once enough tentacles are cleared.

The fight also spawns adds and fires mold projectiles. Staying mobile is critical. I found the scythe form invaluable here because its wide swings let me clear adds without abandoning the tentacle I was working on. When projectiles came in, an air dodge or wall-run reposition kept me out of trouble.

<ProTip title="Do not ignore the adds entirely">The Collective's summoned enemies will chip away at your health while you focus on tentacles. A quick scythe sweep or area ability keeps them manageable, but do not spend so long on them that you lose track of the tentacle timer.</ProTip>

## Ability Synergies and Status Combos

Combat Abilities are not just for Falter. They also apply status effects that can combo with each other. The most commonly cited example in early coverage is infect plus burn causing an explosion. Whether this is the strongest combo in the full game remains to be seen, but the principle is worth internalizing early: abilities that leave a status on an enemy can be detonated by a second status for bonus damage.

You can equip two loadouts of three abilities and swap between them at any time. This lets you run one loadout optimized for traversal or single-target burst and another for crowd control or elemental combos. I kept one loadout focused on Falter buildup and another on area damage, switching based on whether I was entering a boss room or a packed street.

## Day-One Priority Checklist

If you only have an hour or two and want to set yourself up for a smooth run, do these things in order.

1. Finish the prologue. It teaches the loop, the Aberrant, and The Gap without overwhelming you.
2. Pick one primary Aberrant form and one secondary form. Upgrade them before branching out.
3. Learn Perfect Dodge timing against the first large enemy you meet. It pays off for the entire game.
4. Enter The Gap between zones to respec, test combos, and equip Artifacts.
5. Clear zone side content before chasing the next main story marker. Zone XP is your main power source.
6. Target elites first in group fights. Grunts will stop respawning once the big threat is down.
7. Collect health upgrade pieces. Three pieces equal a permanent health increase.
8. Experiment with ability loadouts. Keep one for single-target burst and one for crowds.

## Should You Buy It?

CONTROL Resonant is not a safe sequel. It abandons the gunplay that defined the first game, pushes melee aggression hard, and wraps everything in Remedy's usual love of strange architecture and paranatural lore. For players who enjoy action RPGs like God of War Ragnarök or stylish melee games like Devil May Cry, the loop is immediately satisfying. For players who preferred Control's ranged gun forms and Metroidvania pacing, the open districts and melee focus may feel like a departure.

In my first hours, I landed firmly on the positive side of that split. The moment the Aberrant's combo chain clicked, and I executed an enemy into a Dominance window that cleared the rest of the room, I felt the same flow state Remedy chased in Alan Wake 2's combat. The world is one of the best-looking environments of the generation, and Dylan's movement kit makes simply traveling through warped Manhattan a pleasure. If the full game maintains that energy across its 30-hour campaign, it will be one of the year's most distinctive action RPGs.
