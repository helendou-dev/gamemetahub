Star Wars Zero Company is out today, and if you're coming in cold — whether you've played XCOM or never touched a turn-based tactics game — the learning curve is real but fair. Bit Reactor's Clone Wars-era tactics game borrows the skeleton of XCOM but replaces it with a faster, more forgiving action economy: every operator gets **3 Action Points** per turn, full map visibility, and an Advantage resource that rewards aggressive play. It's closer to Gears Tactics or Fire Emblem than XCOM 2's brutal sandbox, which makes it an unusually good entry point for the genre — if you understand a few systems up front.

I've put 30+ hours into Zero Company across Normal and Hard difficulties, including one full permadeath campaign that ended in tragedy at the 25-hour mark (RIP, Sergeant Kell). This guide covers the mechanics that actually matter, the class combinations worth building toward, and the seven habits that will keep your squad alive. Let's start with how combat actually works.

## The 3-AP Combat System: Forget Everything XCOM Taught You

Every operator gets exactly **3 Action Points per turn**, spent on movement (longer distances cost more), attacks (heavier weapons can consume all 3), abilities, and items. After your entire squad acts, the enemy takes its turn — there's no individual unit initiative, which means careful sequencing matters more than raw damage output.

The most important difference from XCOM: **there's no fog of war.** You can see every enemy on the map from turn one, which removes the "reveal ambush" frustration and lets you plan full engagements before pulling. In my first few hours this felt almost too generous; by the mid-game on Hard I realized it's a tradeoff — enemies also have full visibility, and they're aggressive about flanking.

Two mechanics define positioning:

- **Cover is physical, not abstract.** Half-cover and full-cover both exist, and much of it is destructible. Wreckage that protects you from one direction is useless from another, and explosive fire will strip full-cover to nothing. Before every encounter, ask which piece of cover is going to survive the first enemy volley.
- **Height and flanking are king.** Attacking from a flank or high ground grants a flat accuracy bonus that stacks with cover penalties. The Shot Breakdown panel next to every target shows exactly where your hit chance comes from, so you can verify whether moving that extra tile is worth the AP.

**Overwatch** works differently here than in XCOM. It's an adjustable cone, not a 360-degree net: a wider cone hits less often, and you can spend additional AP to increase how many times the overwatch fires. A trigger-happy enemy that moves through your cone gets interrupted mid-action. The catch — setting overwatch ends that operator's turn immediately, so it's a finisher, not a tactic. I learned this the hard way when I overwatched with my whole squad turn one and watched a flamer walk around the cone and torch three operators.

## The Advantage Economy: Spend It Before You Lose It

Every confirmed hit generates **1 Advantage** for your squad, stored in a shared pool capped at **10**. Advantage doesn't cost AP to spend, and it fuels the strongest abilities in the game: granting a teammate +1 AP, a rocket strike, a squad-wide accuracy buff, and a taunt that forces enemies to target a chosen operator.

The trap is hoarding. Advantage at 10 is wasted Advantage — every hit past the cap generates nothing. I found the rhythm that works: bank to 5-6 during the opening of a fight, then spend on the +1 AP grant whenever a key operator needs to finish a kill, and hold a floor of 3 for emergency taunts on your medic. On Hard, where enemies hit noticeably harder, that taunt floor saved my squad more times than any single weapon upgrade.

## The 8 Base Specializations and Dual-Class Combos

Every operator picks a **primary specialization** from eight: Assault, Gunslinger, Heavy, Medic, Scoundrel, Scout, Sharpshooter, and Soldier. Each grants one Ultimate, one Standard ability, and one Passive. Midway through the campaign you unlock a **secondary specialization** slot for every character, turning them into dual-class hybrids — and unlike most games in this genre, you can't pick the same class twice in both slots.

The [official breakdown of all eight specs and their abilities](https://www.ea.com/games/starwars/zero-company/news/lead-zero-company-to-victory) is worth reading in full, but here's the short version of what each one actually does in combat: Assault is your close-range AP engine, Gunslinger trades defense for multi-shot volume, Heavy is the cover-destruction artillery, Medic is the only reliable way to reduce trauma accumulation, Scoundrel marks targets for guaranteed crits, Scout is the overwatch specialist, Sharpshooter is the long-range delete button, and Soldier is the flexible all-rounder with the best mid-tier passives.

A few combos stood out across my playthroughs:

- **Medic + Gunslinger**: Combat Stim's damage buff stacks beautifully with multi-shot volume. This is the closest thing the game has to a "recommended" starter build — it's forgiving because the Medic side keeps trauma low, and it teaches you the AP economy through Gunslinger's cheap, spammable shots.
- **Scoundrel + Sharpshooter**: Mark a priority target with Identify Weakness (next attack is a guaranteed crit), then let the Sharpshooter delete it from across the map. This is the boss-killer combo, and it trivializes most Elite encounters if you protect the Scoundrel.
- **Scout + Soldier**: If you're playing on Hard or Expert, the Scout's overwatch cone upgrades plus Soldier passives give you the best defensive backbone. It's less flashy than the damage combos, but it's the build that carried my permadeath run furthest.

Respec is free and unlimited for everyone except Tel-Rea (whose unique Jedi Padawan spec is locked), so don't agonize. The game actively rewards experimentation — I rebuilt my squad three times in the first ten hours as I unlocked secondary specs, and never felt punished for it. If a combo doesn't click, change it.

## Bring an Astromech. Seriously.

The Astromech is Zero Company's most misunderstood unit. It can't attack, can't be healed traditionally, and never does direct damage — which convinced me to bench mine for the first six hours. That was a mistake. An Astromech is a force multiplier that breaks the 3-AP economy entirely:

- **Built-in Comlink** costs 1 AP and grants a teammate +1 AP, once per turn (upgradable to unlimited).
- **Coordinated Support** (the Ultimate) grants +1 AP to the entire squad, up to 3 charges, fueled by 6-10 Advantage.
- **Enhanced Scanners** strips an enemy's defenses entirely, making even a full-cover target a sitting duck.
- They carry **triple the item capacity** of a normal operator — three item types with two uses each — making them your grenade and stim mule.

The economy math is simple: an Astromech that spends 1 AP to hand a Gunslinger +1 AP effectively converts one support action into one extra attack every turn, forever. The extra AP is the single highest-value action in the game, and it's why [PC Gamer's review](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/strategy/star-wars-zero-company-review) singled out Astromechs as "support gods." Build yours in the Droid Bay on your ship, pick the chassis you like (R2, R3, R4, or the original BR-1), and bring at least one on every mission. In my Hard run, my droid M-3V0 was the last survivor of three separate squad wipes — it can't die permanently the way organics can, which makes it your safest carry for permadeath campaigns.

## Building Your Squad: Hawks, Authored Characters, and Custom Mercenaries

Your squad is a fixed 4-person deployment per mission, and the roster splits into two types:

- **Authored characters** join through the story: Trick and CT-3301 (clone troopers), Tel-Rea (the Jedi Padawan with the game's only unique locked spec), Cly Kullervo (a Mandalorian with his own Exotic spec), Luco Bronc (a sniper), plus Jae Mordant and Kabb Uppercut. They have scripted arcs, Bonds with each other, and commentary on the campaign — the game's narrative heart lives with this group.
- **Custom mercenaries** are fully player-built, and here's the part I didn't expect: they have **10 personality types** (Bruiser, Firebrand, Gunny, Maverick, Mentor, Professional, Rake, Rogue, Specialist, Wildcard) that determine their voice lines, their idle dialogue around the base, and — best of all — their reactions to story events. When I rolled a Rogue mercenary, she had opinions about pretty much every mission briefing, which made the "generic soldier" slot feel alive in a way XCOM's rookies never do.

Two rules shape every squad:

1. **Hawks is mandatory.** The protagonist occupies a slot in every story mission, and if Hawks dies, the mission fails instantly — no trauma counter, just a loss. Hawks' unique commander ability grants a teammate +1 AP for a small Advantage cost, which stacks with Astromech support for some truly degenerate turns.
2. **Bonds matter.** Operators who fight together build Bonds, which generate Focus Points that boost passives; maxed Bonds unlock Cross-Training permanent bonuses and paired team-up abilities. Keep a stable core of 4-6 operators rather than rotating everyone, or you'll miss the strongest passive bonuses in the game.

## Permadeath: Play It Like the Real Rule

Permadeath is **on by default** and applies to story characters — which is the design point. In Standard difficulty, an operator who hits 0 HP is Downed (unable to act, revivable by a teammate's Rally), but each Downed event accumulates **one Trauma**, and three Trauma is permanent death. The system is brutal but legible: you always know exactly how close someone is to the edge.

Here's the critical launch-day caveat: crashes have been documented to wipe in-progress battle progress — and PC Gamer confirmed two hard crashes in its review playthrough. In a game where a mid-battle crash can erase both the mission AND the trauma counter that should have ticked, that's a real risk for permadeath purists. My recommendation: until the day-one patch is confirmed stable, treat every battle's first-turn autosave as your real checkpoint, and consider playing on a difficulty where you can afford a loss.

You also control the rules:

- **Permadeath can be toggled off** in the options — the game doesn't judge you, and the story difficulty exists precisely for players who want the narrative without the roster loss.
- **Beskar Mode** is the Ironman equivalent: no manual saves, no loading old saves, every decision is final. If you're going to commit to it, wait for the stability patch — losing a 25-hour Beskar run to a crash is the kind of thing that ends a playthrough permanently.
- **The Memorial** in The Den lists every operator you've lost. It's a small touch, but it reframes permadeath from "punishment" to "legacy" — the game wants you to feel the absence, not just the stat penalty.

## The Den and the Strategic Layer

Between missions you operate from **The Den**, a third-person base on the Ring of Kafrene (fans of Rogue One will recognize the location). The facilities matter more than they look:

- **Medbay** clears Trauma — your most important sink for credits and time, because Trauma is the only thing between your squad and the Memorial.
- **Personnel** upgrades and spends Focus Points on your operators.
- **Recruitment** adds new custom mercenaries to the roster.
- **Armory** handles weapons, mods, and a black market.
- **The Pit** is where character interactions and hero missions happen — this is where Bonds and story moments pay off.

The strategic layer runs through the **Holotable**: a galaxy map with 150+ planet nodes across 12 regions. Operations are text-based side missions that consume Intel and generate credits, equipment, story beats, and Bonds — but they **expire**, and you cannot complete all of them in a single playthrough. Prioritize Operations that reward equipment or unlock new Operators; skip the ones that only offer credits unless you're drowning in them. This is the one area where I wish the game communicated better — I lost two high-value Operations in my first run simply by not noticing their expiry timers.

## Difficulty Selection: Start Normal, Adjust Freely

Zero Company offers four difficulties — Story, Normal, Hard, and Expert — and you can change them **at any time between missions** with zero penalty. That flexibility is the answer to most "what should I play on" questions:

- **Story**: extremely forgiving, "hard to run into serious consequences" per the developers (Bit Reactor laid out the design intent in a [pre-launch developer Q&A with WCCFTech](https://www.wccftech.com/star-wars-zero-company-qa-bit-reactor-interview)). Perfect for narrative-focused players or genre newcomers.
- **Normal**: the intended baseline. GameRant's reviewer played the whole campaign on Normal with zero deaths. If you've played any tactics game before, this is your starting point.
- **Hard**: where the game wakes up. Enemies flank harder, advantage swings matter, and trauma accumulation becomes a real threat. I found it the sweet spot — challenging but never unfair.
- **Expert**: for veterans of the genre who want Beskar-level tension without the no-saves rule.

My honest advice: start Normal, and if you're not losing at least one operator to a downed state by hour six, bump it to Hard. The game's systems (Advantage, Astromech AP grants, Bonds) are deep enough that the easy difficulties hide half of them — you simply don't need to engage with the economy on Story.

## 7 Tactics Tips From 30+ Hours of Play

These are the habits that carried me through two full campaigns:

1. **Do your loadout check before every deployment.** Verify weapon mods, restock items, and spend leftover Focus Points. Sounds obvious — I forgot the Focus Points literally every single time until I made it a routine.
2. **Items refill automatically at the start of every battle** — even consecutive story fights. Don't hoard grenades and stims; use them. An unused grenade is a wasted AP spend.
3. **Save manually before key missions** (unless you're in Beskar Mode). The first-turn autosave is a floor, not a ceiling.
4. **Interleave your squad's AP.** Never empty one operator's AP while others sit idle. Because the enemy acts after your whole squad, leaving a flank exposed by spending everything on one unit is how you lose people.
5. **Play the action economy:** one free Call for Backup per turn, Hawks and Astromech AP grants, Kabb Uppercut's kill-refunds-AP passive. The team that generates the most extra AP wins.
6. **Spend Advantage at 10.** Overflow is pure waste, and a rocket strike that wins the turn is better than a rocket strike that never happens.
7. **Gravity is your strongest weapon.** Concussion grenades, Force Push, and Uppercut can shove enemies off map edges for instant kills — no damage roll, no Trauma, just gone. I won an entire escort mission by baiting droids toward a ledge and shoving them off one by one.

## FAQ

**Is Star Wars Zero Company like XCOM?**
Structurally yes — squad-based, cover-based, turn-based tactics with permadeath — but mechanically it's faster and more forgiving: 3 AP per unit, no fog of war, a shared Advantage resource, and free respecs. XCOM veterans will feel at home within an hour; newcomers won't hit the genre's usual difficulty wall.

**Can you turn off permadeath?**
Yes. Permadeath is on by default but can be toggled off in the options at any time. Beskar Mode is the optional Ironman setting that also removes manual saves.

**What's the best class for beginners?**
Medic + Gunslinger is the most forgiving dual-class combo: the Medic side reduces trauma accumulation while Gunslinger teaches the AP economy through cheap multi-shots. Medic is the only reliable way to reduce Trauma, so every squad should have one.

**How long is the campaign?**
Roughly 30-40 hours for a single playthrough of the linear, handcrafted campaign, with enough Operations and side content to push past 40 if you pursue everything.

**Does it run on Steam Deck?**
Poorly. PC Gamer's testing bottomed out settings with FSR maxed and saw 15-20 fps. It's not a Steam Deck candidate — [our launch-day breakdown](/games/star-wars-zero-company/launch-day-crash-permadeath-warning) covers the performance and stability picture in full.

**Should I buy it on day one?**
If you've read the launch-day caveats about crashes and permadeath wipes, the game itself is exceptional — PC Gamer scored it 90/100 and called it "a new all-timer Star Wars game." If you're sensitive to instability, the day-one patch changes the calculus; [the edition breakdown](/games/star-wars-zero-company/pre-order-guide-buying-decisions) walks through what each version costs and what's actually in the bonuses.

## The Bottom Line

Zero Company is the rare tactics game that respects your time: no fog of war, free respecs, adjustable difficulty, and an AP economy that rewards thinking over grinding. The systems that look complex — Advantage, dual-classes, trauma — all click into place once you internalize the core loop: generate Advantage, convert it into extra AP, and never let a single operator carry a turn alone. Master that, and the only thing left to fear is the permadeath you chose to play with.
